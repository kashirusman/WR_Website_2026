The Challenge

Infrastructure investment without behaviour change is infrastructure that fails slowly. The World Bank's WASH programme in Punjab represents an enormous investment in physical assets — clean water systems, proper drainage, sanitation facilities, waste collection infrastructure. But the research evidence on WASH programmes globally is clear: infrastructure alone rarely produces lasting behaviour change. Communities adopt, use incorrectly, or gradually abandon services when the behaviour change component is absent or inadequate.

"A tap that is never turned on is not clean water. A latrine that is used only occasionally is not sanitation. The behaviour is the programme. Everything else is the precondition."

White Rice's challenge is to design a behaviour change programme that operates across multiple domains simultaneously — WASH practices, early childhood development and nutrition, and the financial sustainability of the services through community tariff payment — in 2,000 villages with a single, coherent architecture. The programme must be deliverable by community-level agents of change, who themselves need to be trained, motivated, and equipped to sustain this work over a two-year implementation period.

The additional complexity is the inter-relationship between the behaviour domains. WASH practices connect directly to child health outcomes. Child health outcomes connect to stunting. Stunting connects to early childhood nutrition and stimulation. Service sustainability connects to community ownership — which connects to all of the above. The strategy must hold all of this together without overwhelming either the communities or the frontline workers who will deliver it.

Four Focus Areas · Integrated SBC Architecture

WASH. Nutrition. Child Development.
Service Sustainability. Together.

WASH
Water, Sanitation & Hygiene
Turning the World Bank's WASH infrastructure investment into lasting community behaviour — ensuring that clean water, sanitation facilities, and hygiene services are used consistently, maintained by communities, and passed to the next generation as normal life practice.
Handwashing with soap at critical moments — before eating, after defecation, before feeding children
Consistent latrine use and end of open defecation across all community members
Safe drinking water treatment, storage, and use at household level
Household and community waste disposal through organised collection systems
ECD & Nutrition
Early Childhood Development & Stunting Prevention
Going beyond WASH to address child stunting — Punjab's most pressing child health challenge. The SBC programme integrates nutrition, feeding practice, and early childhood stimulation across the same community infrastructure being built for WASH, reaching caregivers at the moments that matter most.
Diverse diet and appropriate complementary feeding from 6 months — addressing food diversity, not just quantity
Exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months — addressing mixed feeding practices that increase stunting risk
Early childhood stimulation — responsive play, reading, conversation — particularly targeting fathers as active participants
Antenatal and postnatal nutrition practices — reaching mothers before, during, and after pregnancy
Service Sustainability
Tariff Payment & Community Ownership
The financial sustainability of WASH services depends on communities understanding and accepting the value of paying for them. This is a behaviour change challenge — shifting the perception of water and sanitation from a government gift to a community-owned service worth maintaining and paying for.
Regular, timely tariff payment — building the financial habit that makes PRMAC's operations viable
Perceived value of services — helping communities understand the economic and health returns on their tariff investment
Community stewardship — the sense of ownership that makes communities report problems, maintain facilities, and advocate for service quality
Community Norms
Exemplary Village Status & Social Identity
The programme's highest ambition — to make these 2,000 villages exemplary. Communities that others look to. Villages where WASH is not a programme outcome but an identity. Where early childhood development is a community priority. Where paying for services is a matter of pride, not obligation.
Village-level pride and competition around cleanliness, child health outcomes, and service adoption
Community leaders and influencers as champions — actively reinforcing norms from within
Intergenerational transfer — children growing up in programme villages as the new normal baseline

The Approach

White Rice's approach to this programme is grounded in the principle that communities are not passive recipients of behaviour change — they are the designers of it. The SBC strategy is being co-created with communities and partners on the ground, ensuring that every session, every material, and every engagement tool reflects the actual context, language, concerns, and aspirations of the villages it is designed for.

The integration of WASH, ECD, nutrition, and tariff sustainability in a single SBC architecture is deliberate — because these challenges are not separate in communities. A mother who learns proper handwashing is also learning about child health. A father who attends an ECD session is also hearing about the water system his family depends on. A community that takes pride in its cleanliness is a community that will pay to maintain it. The strategy treats these connections as assets, not complications.
